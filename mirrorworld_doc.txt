2. Abstract

Universities make resource decisions, such as adding students or moving laboratories,
without a way to test the consequences first. MIRRORWORLD is a campus digital twin
that models rooms, labs, teachers and student groups with real operational constraints.
Administrators create what-if scenarios (for example, +200 students or a lab closure), and
the platform simulates the impact, generates alternative timetables with an OR-Tools
optimizer, and compares them with the baseline on conflicts, room utilization, energy
use and teacher workload, then explains the trade-offs. It supports SDG 9, 11 and 12.

 3. Problem Statement

  University timetabling and resource planning are tightly coupled problems. Moving one
  laboratory session can affect teacher availability, room availability, student schedules,
  equipment use, congestion and electricity consumption at the same time. In practice,
  administrators often rely on manual calculation, experience or trial and error, and discover
  problems only after the change is implemented.
  Specific gaps:

      Management systems store records but do not let users experiment with future
      states.
      Timetable generators produce a schedule but rarely explain wider consequences or
      trade-offs.
      There is little support for comparing several alternatives against a measured baseline.
      Sudden disruptions (room closure, teacher absence, enrollment growth) are handled
      manually.
      Energy and utilization impact is rarely considered during scheduling.

 4. Proposed Solution and Objectives

  Solution. A configurable digital model of a university campus, combined with a simulation
  engine, a constraint-based optimizer, a dependency-impact analyzer and an explainable
  comparison interface.
  Objectives
   1. Build a structured digital model of campus resources and operational rules.
   2. Let users create counterfactual scenarios without altering the baseline.
   3. Generate valid alternative timetables through constraint optimization.
   4. Quantify outcomes with transparent mathematical metrics.
   5. Explain trade-offs and unresolved constraints in plain language.
   6. Support disruption recovery when a resource becomes unavailable.
   7. Record every experiment for reproducibility and audit.
  The five guiding questions: What is happening now? What happens if we change X? Which
  constraints or resources become problematic? Which alternative performs best? Why,
  and at what cost?

5. Novelty and Differentiation

Digital twins and timetable optimization are established fields, so MIRRORWORLD does
not claim to invent them. Its novelty is the integrated combination of four capabilities in
one decision workflow:

1. Counterfactual simulation: change one condition, keep the rest constant, and
    measure the effect against the baseline.

2. Dependency-aware impact analysis: a resource graph identifies exactly which
    teachers, courses, groups and rooms are affected by a change.

3. Multi-objective optimization: several plans (utilization-first, energy-first, balanced)
    are generated and compared, with a Pareto view of trade-offs.

4. Explainable recommendations: explanations are generated from computed results,
    not from a chatbot.

Before final submission, review existing campus scheduling and digital-twin tools and
state precisely how this implementation differs.

7.2 Request flow (one experiment)

1. Frontend sends a scenario definition (parameter changes) to the API.
2. API validates input with Pydantic schemas and checks data consistency.
3. Scenario service loads the baseline from PostgreSQL and creates a deep copy with the

    changes applied.
4. Simulation engine evaluates the scenario and computes indicators.
5. Optimization engine runs with a time limit and returns one or more candidate

    timetables, or reports infeasibility.
6. Metrics service computes all indicators for each candidate and the baseline.
7. Explanation service compares results and produces structured explanations.
8. Results, assumptions and warnings are stored in the audit log and returned to the UI.

Long-running optimization runs are executed as background jobs (FastAPI background
tasks or a lightweight worker such as RQ/Celery with Redis). The frontend polls a status
endpoint and shows progress.

7.3 Key design principles

    Baseline is immutable; every experiment is a separate scenario record.
    No fabricated numbers: every displayed value comes from the engine and is traceable.
    Data is labeled as Observed, Estimated, Simulated or Optimized.
    Hard constraints are enforced in the solver, not penalized.
    Deterministic runs: the same input and random seed produce the same output.

11. Mathematical Model

11.1 Room utilization

U = (Occupied room-hours / Available room-hours) × 100%
Example: 30 occupied hours out of 40 available gives U = 75%. High utilization is not
automatically good; some slack is needed for flexibility.

11.2 Capacity violation

V_i = max(0, S_i - K_i), where S_i is the number of students in class i and K_i is the
capacity of the assigned room. Total violation = sum of V_i. Valid plans require this to be 0.

11.3 Scheduling conflicts

A conflict is counted when two sessions that share a teacher, room or student group
occupy the same time slot. In the optimizer these are forbidden by hard constraints; in the
baseline and in disrupted scenarios they are counted as diagnostics.

 11.4 Energy estimate

  E = sum over rooms r of (P_r × H_r), where P_r is the modeled power load in kW and H_r
  the operating hours. A refined model separates lighting, air conditioning, computers and
  laboratory equipment. Assumptions are documented and kept identical across scenarios.
  The model does not capture weather or real air-conditioning behavior, and this is stated as
  a limitation.

 11.5 Teacher workload balance

  W = standard deviation of weekly teaching hours across teachers (lower is more
  balanced), also reporting maximum load against each teacher's limit.

 11.6 Student movement

  M = sum over consecutive session pairs of a group of d(building_a, building_b), where d is
  a building-distance (or transition-penalty) matrix.

 11.7 Objective function

  Minimize Z = w1·C + w2·E + w3·M + w4·W + w5·G, where C is soft-constraint violations, E
  is normalized energy, M is movement, W is workload imbalance, G is schedule gaps, and w
  are user-adjustable weights. Each term is normalized before weighting so the weights are
  comparable.

 12. Optimization Engine (OR-Tools CP-SAT)

  Decision variables: x[s, r, t]  {0,1} equals 1 if session s is placed in room r at time slot t.
  Hard constraints
   1. Every session is assigned exactly once (or reported as unassigned in a relaxed run).
   2. A room hosts at most one session per slot.
   3. A teacher teaches at most one session per slot and only in available slots.
   4. A student group attends at most one session per slot.
   5. Group size must not exceed room capacity.
   6. Required equipment and lab type must match the room.
   7. Rooms and slots marked unavailable are excluded.
   8. Teacher weekly hours must not exceed the maximum.

                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  Page 11 of 17

   9. Multi-slot sessions (labs) occupy consecutive slots.
  Soft constraints (penalized in the objective): gaps between classes, long inter-building
  movement, workload imbalance, peak-hour congestion, energy-inefficient room choices,
  preferred lab time windows.
  Behavior: a time limit is set for each solve; the solver status (OPTIMAL, FEASIBLE,
  INFEASIBLE) is reported honestly. If infeasible, the system explains which constraint
  groups conflict using relaxed re-runs. Plans A-D are produced by re-solving with different
  weight sets.

 13. Simulation Engine

      Static evaluation: computes all metrics for any given timetable and campus
      configuration.
      Counterfactual run: clones the baseline, applies parameter changes (for example,
      +200 students split proportionally across groups), and re-evaluates.
      Discrete-event simulation (SimPy): models student movement between sessions,
      corridor or room queues and peak congestion within a day, using a documented
      process model.
      Disruption module: removes a resource, uses the dependency graph to find affected
      sessions, tries local repair first, then a full re-optimization, and reports unresolved
      items.
      Reproducibility: fixed random seeds and stored parameters.

 14. Dependency Graph and Impact Analysis

  Nodes: teachers, courses, sessions, student groups, rooms, buildings, equipment. Edges:
  teaches, attends, assigned-to, located-in, requires. Implemented with NetworkX; traversal
  (BFS) from a disrupted node returns affected sessions, teachers and groups, and
  candidate alternative rooms and slots. Results are rendered in React Flow.

 15. Explanation Engine

  Rule-based templates fed by computed metric differences. Example logic: if conflicts fall
  and afternoon utilization rises, state that conflicts were reduced by spreading laboratory
  sessions across more slots at the cost of less afternoon flexibility. Each statement

16. Demonstration Dataset and Baseline

    Synthetic but realistic campus: about 1,000 students, 20 classrooms, 4 laboratories,
    30-40 teachers, 25-30 student groups, a weekly timetable of roughly 100 sessions.
    Baseline deliberately contains a few real conflicts and overloaded rooms so
    improvements are measurable.
    Parameters (capacities, power ratings, distances) documented in an assumptions
    table.
    If real university data is later available, import via CSV.

21. Live Demonstration Script (5 minutes)

1. Show the baseline campus and its KPIs (1,000 students, 20 rooms, 4 labs).
2. Increase students to 1,200 and click Simulate Future; show the new capacity and

    conflict problems.
3. Click Find Better Plans; show at least two feasible plans (utilization-first and

    balanced).
4. Open the comparison view: metrics, trade-offs, Pareto chart and revised timetable.
5. Show the explanation of why the recommended plan is better and what it sacrifices.
6. Let a judge pick a condition (for example, close a lab) and run recovery live; show

    affected resources in the graph and the new arrangement.
7. Keep a prepared, tested dataset and a recorded backup video in case of network or

    hardware issues.

22. Limitations and Future Work

    Uses a synthetic dataset unless real campus data is supplied.
    Energy values are estimates; occupancy, weather and air-conditioning behavior are not
    modeled.
    Student movement uses a simplified distance model.
    Future work: integration with real timetable systems (CSV/API), live IoT energy data,
    richer sensitivity analysis, multi-campus support, learning-based preference tuning
    once real data exists.
